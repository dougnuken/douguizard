This is how I work, in five steps.

First I want to see the real work: the files people use today. Exceptions are where the design is hard.

Then I map states and roles. At Naowee I mapped more than 45 states and 15 roles before I drew a screen.

Then a prototype with real roles. Then the system. Then I ship a safe version and I listen.

Requirements and collaboration

The demo is the requirement, not the document.

How I gather requirements

  • Start from the artifacts. The forms, files and emails people use today show the real process.
  • Attack the plan early. At DC, an adversarial review on data, security and feasibility; only the third version reached code.
  • Sign off on something running. Specs get read differently by everyone; a prototype surfaces disagreement while it is cheap.

How I work with engineering

  • A resolved target, not a spec. Engineering gets a prototype built on the system's components.
  • One component API. At Mercadolibre we agreed single APIs across iOS, Android and Web.
  • Rules as tests. At DC, balances, card fees, dates and name matching are tested functions: 12 suites.

For requirements, I start from the real artifacts.

I also attack the plan early. At DC Medical the plan went through a hard review on data, security and feasibility. Only the third version reached code.

I like to sign off on something that runs. People read documents in different ways. A prototype shows the disagreement early, when it is cheap.

With engineers, I give a resolved target, built with the system. And I turn business rules into tests.

The problem

Every squad solved the same problem differently.

The portal was dense and buried people in nested pages. Fixing screens alone would not last: the fix had to be a system, and someone had to own it.

The old portal was dense. People got lost in nested pages.

And every squad built its own answer to the same problem.

Fixing screens was not enough. We needed a system, and an owner.

UX moment 1

In banking, the exception is the product.

What we did
Discovery and user research first. Then six flows, mapped with their exceptions before any screen.
How we validated
Every stage became a prototype tested on people before build; registration went through several rounds.
The trade-off
A slower start, and fewer surprises in build.

In Hyperproof terms Mapping the rejection and failure paths of a workflow before building it.

The happy path is easy. The failures are the real design.

Here is registration with a one-time password. Look at the failure branches: resend, wrong code, validation failure.

I drew them first. Then we tested prototypes with users before we built.

UX moment 2

Two layers of navigation, and no more.

The pain
The old portal buried people in nested pages.
What we did
A popup system replaced navigation depth. Two layers cover every banking task.
The trade-off
A hard limit forces teams to simplify their flows. Simple rules get followed.

I set a hard limit: two layers.

Page, blur, component, popup. Every task fits inside it.

The rule is simple, so the squads follow it.

UX moment 3

Identical across devices, not merely similar.

The pain
The brief asked for a “similar” experience. Similar means relearning.
What we did
Every desktop capability reaches the phone, with the same names, in the same order.
The trade-off
The phone carries more, but nothing is relearned on the bus.

The brief said "similar". I made it identical.

Same names, same order, on the phone and on the desktop.

People do not relearn anything when they change device.

I owned the gate. Teams could propose additions, but each change went through review.

Velocity is indexed the way the front end is built: foundations, atoms, molecules, organisms.

Each new screen cost less than the last one.
